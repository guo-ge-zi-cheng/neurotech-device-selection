## Layer 1: Original research

Original snapshot: 19 October 2026, with earlier targeted additions on 20 October (ARC-EX was added on 20 October). The text and access labels below are preserved. Read Layer 2 for new evidence and dated updates.

### What does it do?

The RNS System is an implanted treatment for selected people with drug-resistant focal epilepsy. Electrodes are placed at or near one or two identified seizure sources. They connect to a neurostimulator implanted in the skull. The system records local brain electrical activity and looks for patterns that the treating team has programmed it to detect.

When a programmed pattern is detected, the device delivers a short electrical stimulation. This is the meaning of responsive or closed-loop treatment: the recorded signal affects when stimulation is delivered. It does not need to wait for a person to press a button. The clinician still sets and adjusts the detection and stimulation parameters; the device does not independently decide every aspect of epilepsy care.

Recorded data also give the clinical team a view of activity between appointments. This makes the product both a treatment and a source of long-term intracranial recordings. The case can therefore explain why sensing, stimulation, follow-up and data review have to work together. It should also explain the surgical and monitoring burden and the alternatives available to the same patients.

### How far has it developed?

This is a marketed US implant, with original PMA approval in 2013 and a substantial clinical history. The selected case should remain focal epilepsy. Investigational generalized-epilepsy work is a separate indication, not evidence that the original label covers every epilepsy type.

Sources for this description and stage: [FDA • RNS P100026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf10/P100026B.pdf); [Morrell et al.: responsive cortical stimulation trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21917777/); [Nair et al.: nine-year prospective RNS follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32690786/); [NeuroPace July 2026 FDA update on generalized epilepsy supplement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1528287/000119312526321083/d138221dex991.htm).

#### What does the clinical evidence actually show?

The original 2013 PMA concerns adults with drug-resistant focal/partial-onset seizures, with no more than two localized foci and other label criteria. In the 191-person randomized study, seizure frequency fell 37.9% with active stimulation versus 17.3% with sham during the blinded evaluation. The nine-year study reports a 75% median reduction, but that later figure comes from open-label follow-up, not another nine-year randomized comparison. A July 2026 company FDA update did not establish approval for generalized epilepsy.

**Practical takeaway:** One of the clearer choices for public research: trials, an early patent, a detailed prospectus and an economic model are already identified. Early financing still needs careful extraction.

## Layer 2: Additional research (20 October 2026)

A pre-approval securities filing and an actual payer policy add more than another general company summary. The new layer also distinguishes broad epilepsy burden from RNS eligibility and separates MRI labels by model.

**How to read this layer:** each finding states what the evidence supports, why it is usable, how it helps the report and where it stops. [Reliability protocol](../docs/reliability-protocol.md). “Context” is useful background, not a result for this product. No new interview has taken place.

### 1. Unmet medical need

**Search route:** Original government population survey, with an explicit eligibility boundary.

#### A dated US burden denominator

**Context.** Kobau et al. report an estimated 2,865,000 US adults (1.1%) with active epilepsy in the 2021 NHIS. Active epilepsy uses a reported doctor diagnosis plus current treatment and/or a seizure in the previous year.

**Why this evidence is usable:** This is the authors’ original survey report in the CDC repository. Only the abstract/record was reviewed here, so its reported estimate is used within that coverage.

**Use in the report:** Give the unmet-need section a clear year, country and denominator, then narrow to the drug-resistant focal population and relevant treatment alternatives.

**Limit:** This is not an RNS-eligible patient count and is not a UK estimate. Full survey methods, uncertainty and the eligible fraction need a further read.

Sources: [Kobau et al.: active epilepsy in US adults, 2021 NHIS](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/154714).

**Where checked:** CDC repository abstract and metadata. **Evidence route:** New source route. Checked 2026-10-20; source dates and review coverage are in the [source index](../docs/sources.md).

**Remaining gap:** Quantify the narrower eligible population and before/after care costs using the original clinical/economic sources, not all epilepsy cases.

### 2. Intellectual property

**Search route:** Deeper patent reading plus institutional founder history.

#### What the foundational claim says

**Documented fact.** In the already-listed US6016449A, independent claim 1 combines intracranial electrodes, an implanted under-scalp module that responds to brain inputs with stimulation, and external two-way communication/programming. The publication names Robert Fischell, David Fischell and Adrian Upton.

**Why this evidence is usable:** This is a deeper reading of the original patent source, not a new independent confirmation. The text supports a technical explanation of responsive stimulation.

**Use in the report:** Explain the sensing–decision–stimulation loop and the programming link in plain language before examining later device improvements.

**Limit:** Google metadata indicates lifetime expiry; current enforceability was not audited. This single claim is not the complete independent-claim analysis required by the coursework.

Sources: [US6016449A: implantable seizure warning/treatment system](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6016449A/en).

**Where checked:** Independent claim 1; inventors and bibliographic/legal metadata. **Evidence route:** Deeper check of a previously listed source. Checked 2026-10-20; source dates and review coverage are in the [source index](../docs/sources.md).

#### Founder history does not settle ownership

**Context.** A 2008 Johns Hopkins account identifies Robert Fischell as NeuroPace’s founder and describes his earlier 38-year APL career.

**Why this evidence is usable:** The university account corroborates the biographical relationship. It does not supply a patent assignment or employment contract.

**Use in the report:** Use it to frame an origin timeline and a precise follow-up question about where the invention was made and who owned it.

**Limit:** Do not infer that Johns Hopkins owned or licensed the RNS patent, or employed him when it was filed.

Sources: [Johns Hopkins Engineering: Robert Fischell and NeuroPace history](https://engineering.jhu.edu/magazine-archive/2008/09/fischell-awarded-honorary-degree/).

**Where checked:** University historical article. **Evidence route:** New source route. Checked 2026-10-20; source dates and review coverage are in the [source index](../docs/sources.md).

**Remaining gap:** Reconstruct the original employment/assignment chain and read the other relevant independent claims and current improvement patents.

### 4. Regulatory

**Search route:** Use version-specific safety labeling instead of carrying an old restriction across all models.

**Documented fact.** The February 2020 Rev 6 manufacturer guidelines identify RNS-320 as MR Conditional under specified conditions and RNS-300M as MR Unsafe.

**Why this evidence is usable:** The actual model-specific manual was read. It supplies historical labeling detail rather than an unsourced general claim about implants.

### 5. Reimbursement

**Search route:** Read a payer’s actual coverage criteria alongside the existing economic model.

#### A policy is available, with defined eligibility

**Documented fact.** Aetna CPB 0394, last reviewed 23 June 2026, treats responsive cortical stimulation as medically necessary for selected adults with intractable focal epilepsy under stated conditions. These include no more than two localised epileptogenic regions and adequate trials of at least two antiseizure medicines; the full policy has further surgical/clinical criteria.

**Why this evidence is usable:** Aetna is the payer issuing this policy. This is direct evidence of its coverage position, whereas the earlier cost-effectiveness paper is a model.

**Use in the report:** Explain which clinical evidence a payer asks for and separate coverage from economic value. Map the patient, epilepsy team, implanting centre and insurer.

**Limit:** It is not universal US or NHS coverage, a guaranteed patient benefit, or a payment amount. Read all criteria and plan terms before making a coverage claim.

Sources: [Aetna CPB 0394: epilepsy surgery and responsive cortical stimulation](https://www.aetna.com/cpb/medical/data/300_399/0394.html).

**Where checked:** Responsive Cortical Stimulation section and review date. **Evidence route:** New source route. Checked 2026-10-20; source dates and review coverage are in the [source index](../docs/sources.md).

**Remaining gap:** Find the relevant payment amounts and UK access route separately; a US commercial policy does not answer NHS commissioning.
