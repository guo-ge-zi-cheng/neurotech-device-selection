# Required evidence: the common checklist

Derived from the team's supplied Medical_Device_Report_Guide.pdf, sections 3–7, on 21 October 2026. This is the team's working decomposition; it does not replace Project spec or create a new grading rubric. The PDF remains outside the public repo. Its identity and review scope are recorded in [structured data](../data/evidence-checklists.json).

There are **31 questions per device**: six in each topic, plus the stakeholder map. Every [device research guide](../research/README.md) explains each requirement in full sentences, then distinguishes evidence, what it supports, what remains unanswered and the next investigation. No scale or grade replaces that explanation.

## Unmet medical need

Guide physical PDF pp5–6.

- **Who could actually receive this device? (N1)** Explain the condition and the specific patients or procedures the device is intended for. Then give the number who meet those criteria in a named country and year. Explain why a count of everyone with the disease would include people who cannot receive this device.

- **Where would the device enter existing care? (N2)** Describe what happens to a patient before, during and after the relevant treatment or investigation. Identify the decision the device changes, the usual alternative and the staff or equipment needed at that point.

- **What specifically goes wrong with existing care? (N3)** Show a measured problem with the care available before the device: for example, continuing symptoms, missed diagnoses, waiting time or patients unable to access treatment. Explain how often it happens. A later trial of the new device does not by itself measure the original care shortfall.

- **What are the consequences of that problem? (N4)** Explain how the care shortfall affects patients and services. Connect it to outcomes, staff time, hospital use or costs, and identify which groups carry that burden. A large disease-cost figure needs a reasoned connection to the patients and problem this device addresses.

- **What benefit has this particular device demonstrated? (N5)** Explain how the device could change the identified problem and what has actually been measured. Distinguish a technical result, a change in a clinical decision and an improvement patients experience. State the study population, comparison and follow-up so the reader can see how far the finding reaches.

- **What other options could meet the same need? (N6)** Compare options available to the same patients at the same point in care. Explain their different benefits, risks and practical demands. A product used as a regulatory comparison is not automatically the most appropriate clinical alternative.


## Intellectual property

Guide physical PDF pp6–8.

- **Who made the original invention, and where? (I1)** Identify the inventors of the technology that became the selected device, their employers when the invention arose and the earliest relevant publication or patent priority. A current university biography is not evidence of where an inventor worked at that earlier date.

- **How did the company obtain the right to use it? (I2)** Explain who originally owned the invention and how rights reached the developer through assignment, acquisition or licence. Identify the records supporting each step. A shared founder, research collaboration or patent applicant does not establish every transfer or licence term.

- **Which patent claims correspond to the device's features? (I3)** Explain each relevant independent claim in ordinary language, then compare its required features with documented features of the selected product. An independent claim sets out a combination of features without relying on another claim. Identify any feature that cannot yet be matched.

- **Where and when does that protection apply? (I4)** Identify the relevant patent family, priority date and countries, and check the dated status of the particular application or granted patent. An application, an expired patent and a live grant answer different questions. Later amendments may change the claims.

- **What else can the company protect or control? (I5)** Look for documented software rights, designs, trademarks, confidential know-how or data rights that matter to the device. Explain what the company controls and why it could matter commercially. Owning a factory does not by itself establish that a particular process is legally protected.

- **What could limit that control or allow competition? (I6)** Identify relevant third-party rights, licences on which the developer depends and possible competing designs. Explain what the available records let us say about competition. Our search does not establish a legal right to commercialise every feature or prove freedom from infringement.


## Fundraising

Guide physical PDF pp8–10.

- **Was early research supported by grants? (F1)** For each relevant grant, establish who received it, the award identifier, dates, amount and purpose. Explain whether it supported a precursor laboratory, the company or this product. An acknowledgement in a paper can identify a lead without revealing the money attributable to the device.

- **What money did investors actually provide? (F2)** Reconstruct the relevant early investment: who invested, when it closed, the currency, amount, type of security and any disclosed valuation. Separate money offered from money sold or received, and distinguish a company fundraising total from money allocated to this product.

- **What other resources helped development? (F3)** Investigate founder contributions, loans, convertible instruments, partner support, incubator facilities and in-kind resources. Explain what was actually provided and under what arrangement. Do not count an acquisition the company paid for as investment received by the company.

- **What development work was the money intended to fund? (F4)** Connect the funding to the next prototype, test, trial, manufacturing step or market-entry milestone. State whether the source reports an intention, an allocated budget or actual expenditure. A round announcement alone does not show how the company divided its money.

- **What was delivered after the funding? (F5)** Place funding events and documented development outputs on the same timeline. Identify evidence of a prototype, study, regulatory decision or first sale. Timing can show that funding preceded an outcome, but additional evidence is needed to claim the funding paid for that outcome.

- **What would early development have cost if the budget is private? (F6)** Build a reasoned estimate using both approaches allowed by the specification. Add up sourced staffing, engineering, testing, trial and manufacturing costs from the bottom up, and compare with similar programmes from the top down. State assumptions and ranges. This estimates capital needed; it does not reconstruct undisclosed fundraising.


## Regulatory

Guide physical PDF pp10–12.

- **Exactly which product and use are being regulated? (R1)** Define the hardware, software and accessories, the intended patients, user and care setting. Identify the original version being studied and separate later changes. This prevents evidence about a component or research system being applied to a different complete product.

- **Why did it follow this regulatory route? (R2)** For each market discussed, explain the device classification and the applicable rule or product code, then connect the route to the device's function and risks. Evidence of a US route does not establish UK or EU authorisation.

- **What actual decision allowed market entry? (R3)** Give the decision identifier, date, type and authorised scope from the original record. Distinguish clearance, approval, registration and a manufacturer's market announcement. Check later changes separately if the report makes claims about the present product.

- **What tests and standards supported that decision? (R4)** Connect the device's specific risks to the tests or standards reported in its regulatory record. Identify the edition where available and what the test showed. A general standard explains an expectation; it does not prove the manufacturer performed or passed that test.

- **Who made the device and took responsibility for quality? (R5)** Identify the legal manufacturer, important contract manufacturers or suppliers, production sites and who controlled assembly, testing and release. Distinguish a documented relationship from a supplier that merely has relevant capabilities. State the date of the manufacturing account.

- **What obligations and problems continued after launch? (R6)** Explain the relevant quality and surveillance duties and investigate product-specific recalls or reported incidents. State the database, model, terms and date searched. A general quality rule does not establish company compliance, and a missing search result does not establish that no problem occurred.


## Reimbursement

Guide physical PDF pp13–15.

- **Who buys what, and at what price? (P1)** Describe the actual sale, lease, subscription or consumable arrangement, including the configuration, date and price. Separate the manufacturer's selling price from a hospital charge, a service payment or a modelled treatment cost.

- **Who pays for the patient's care? (P2)** Identify whether the relevant care is funded by a commissioner, insurer, hospital, patient, research grant or charity. Connect that source to a real setting. The perspective chosen by an economic model does not prove that the named payer actually funds the device.

- **Under what rules and amounts is care paid for? (P3)** For a named payer, place, setting and year, separate billing codes, clinical coverage conditions and payment amounts. A code permits classification; it does not on its own promise coverage or a particular payment. Temporary support needs its own dates and conditions.

- **What extra costs and savings can be supported? (P4)** Compare the device pathway with the relevant alternative. Include equipment, implementation, staff, maintenance and downstream events, and state the perspective and time horizon. Explain which amounts are observed and which are model inputs; do not turn a hypothetical saving into a realised one.

- **What clinical evidence supports the economic assumptions? (P5)** Trace each proposed saving or health gain to evidence about an outcome or event frequency. Explain how a change in a measured endpoint leads to fewer admissions, less care or better quality of life. Faster setup or better signals do not automatically establish those later effects.

- **Who has a practical reason to adopt it? (P6)** Identify who controls the budget, who pays the extra costs and who benefits, over what time period. Include staffing, capacity and throughput. A treatment can look worthwhile to the whole health system while leaving the hospital that implements it with an immediate cost.

- **How do the stakeholders and flows fit together? (P7)** Map the verified product, care, information, money and authority flows, and explain the participants' goals or tensions. Distinguish documented relationships from proposed ones. Use the lecture's required map format once it is available rather than assuming our existing diagram matches it.


## Interpret the result

Read what a source establishes and then the specific question it leaves unanswered. A regulatory decision can establish original market entry while leaving later labels unresolved. An economic model can show the authors' assumptions while leaving actual prices unknown. A later patent or another product's funding cannot fill an original-device gap.

Unknown is not the same as false or undisclosed. A proposed query is not a completed search. Database access does not create missing private facts, and an additional source does not automatically close a gap. [Reliability protocol](reliability-protocol.md).
