## Bret Stephens: “I’m Begging You: Never Write With A.I.”

The supplied New York Times opinion article is dated **4 August 2026** (four-page PDF). Stephens treats writing as a practice of thinking: choosing claims, discovering incoherence, taking responsibility and developing an individual voice. Outsourcing the initial composition can remove that practice, even when the resulting prose is competent. His objections also concern authenticity, passing off another source’s work as one’s own, and the wider consequences of weakening literacy and judgement. He concedes that a system can write better than many people, so his argument does not depend entirely on current model incompetence.

This is a normative opinion essay, not a controlled test of learning or an evaluation of this system. The dissertation-detection percentages it cites are not treated here as ground-truth rates of AI authorship: the underlying detector claims were not independently validated. Better model accuracy would not by itself settle the learning or authorship objections. A useful response for this team is to retain responsibility for the argument: each member should read key records and explain why a device is a good choice without relying on polished generated wording.

## Hicks, Humphries and Slater: “ChatGPT is bullshit”

The file named “Reality of ChatGPT.pdf” contains this **2024** paper in *Ethics and Information Technology*, 26:38, DOI 10.1007/s10676-024-09775-5. Its argument and conclusion (PDF pp1–9) were reviewed; its cited studies were not independently replicated. Using Frankfurt’s terminology, the authors argue that fluent output can be produced without a truth-directed commitment. They distinguish “soft” truth-indifference from “hard” cases involving an intention, or an intention-like function, to mislead about the nature of the enterprise. The stronger interpretation depends on disputed assumptions about agency. They object to “hallucination” as a metaphor because it suggests perception going wrong and can obscure responsibility.

The paper also discusses tool-connected systems: retrieval does not guarantee correctness if a model asks the wrong query or misreads the response. It would therefore be inaccurate to dismiss it as discussing only completely isolated chatbots. However, its examples and arguments do not quantify the accuracy of this October 2026 workflow. Nor does its account of next-token prediction alone demonstrate that every possible trained or tool-supported system cannot reason or improve. Those are empirical and conceptual questions requiring more evidence. The actionable point is narrower and useful: fluent claims and plausible citations require verification of the actual record and its meaning.
